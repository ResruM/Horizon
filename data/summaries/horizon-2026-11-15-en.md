### [Study of 102,684 Ash chatbot users finds five engagement phenotypes tied to symptom change](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-78802-w) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A study in Scientific Reports clustered eight behavioral features from 102,684 users of Ash, a purpose-built AI mental health chatbot, into five engagement phenotypes: Early Dropouts \(52.2%\), Weekly Users \(25.3%\), a new Concentrated User pattern \(16.8%\), Intensive Users \(4.1%\) and Power Users \(1.6%\); 66.9% of users had at least one session between 9pm and 5am. In small follow-up subsamples measured at baseline and three weeks, self-reported depression \(PHQ-9, n = 298, d = -0.51\), anxiety \(GAD-7, n = 298, d = -0.57\) and social support \(MSPSS, n = 194, d = 0.22\) improved. A dose-response gradient — largest improvements among Power \(d = -0.57\) and Intensive \(d = -0.50\) users, smallest among Early Dropouts \(d = -0.22\) — was replicated in a larger sample using model-predicted PHQ-9 \(n = 23,813\). The authors characterize the findings as naturalistic and correlational, caution against treating session counts as a primary engagement metric, and disclose that four authors are employees of the company building Ash.

**「Why engagement patterns matter」** Digital mental health research has typically measured engagement with coarse metrics such as session counts, retention, or dropout, which treat use as a single quantity rather than a set of distinct behavioral patterns. Evidence on conversational AI mental health tools has also come mainly from smaller, controlled settings, leaving real-world usage patterns and their relationship to symptom change largely uncharacterized. This study addresses that gap by applying k-means clustering to eight behavioral features from a large user sample and then relating the resulting clusters to pre-post clinical measures.

**「Impact」** For clinicians and product teams evaluating AI mental health tools, the results argue for measuring engagement pattern and therapeutic alliance — 11,437 users completed a baseline Working Alliance Inventory, and higher alliance predicted depression improvement and moderated the engagement-social support relationship — rather than raw session counts. The three-week follow-up and small clinical subsamples \(n = 298 or fewer\) mean the reported effect sizes cannot establish that chatbot use caused the improvements, and the authors call for controlled designs to confirm them.
