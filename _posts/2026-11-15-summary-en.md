### [Study maps five engagement types among 102,684 Ash chatbot users](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-78802-w) ⭐️ 7.0/10

A peer-reviewed analysis of 102,684 users of the AI mental health chatbot Ash used k-means clustering on eight behavioral features to identify five engagement phenotypes: Early Dropouts \(52.2%\), Weekly Users \(25.3%\), a Concentrated User pattern \(16.8%\), Intensive Users \(4.1%\), and Power Users \(1.6%\); 66.9% had at least one session between 9 p.m. and 5 a.m. Among smaller clinical subsamples assessed at baseline and three weeks, depression improved \(d = −0.51; PHQ-9, n = 298\), anxiety improved \(d = −0.57; GAD-7, n = 298\), and social support rose \(d = 0.22; MSPSS, n = 194\). A dose-response gradient showed larger depression improvement for Power Users \(d = −0.57\) and Intensive Users \(d = −0.50\) than for Early Dropouts \(d = −0.22\), replicated using model-predicted PHQ-9 in 23,813 users; higher working alliance predicted depression improvement. The design was observational and pre-post without a control group, so the associations are correlational and the clinical subsamples were small.

rss · Nature Psychology · Nov 15, 00:00

**「Background」** Ash is a purpose-built AI mental health chatbot from Slingshot AI, a research lab that describes its work as building a foundation model for psychology rather than adapting a general-purpose assistant to the task \(tool-2-1, tool-2-2\). The study draws on behavioral data from Ash&\#x27;s own user base, and four of its authors — CAS, TS, OT, and TDH — are identified in the paper as employees of the company building the model, a relationship disclosed under competing interests.

**「Impact」** For developers and researchers evaluating mental health chatbots, the findings suggest engagement should be measured by phenotype and clinical outcome rather than session counts alone; the authors caution against treating session counts as a primary engagement metric. Because the clinical analyses were uncontrolled and based on 298 or fewer users, the reported effect sizes should be treated as hypotheses for controlled trials, not as demonstrated efficacy.
